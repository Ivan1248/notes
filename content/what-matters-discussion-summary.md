## "What Matters?" — Summary of a Discussion on Value-Facts, Metaethics, and Consciousness

_Written by Claude Opus 5.5._ _Slanted responses and highlighting by Ivan._

This document summarizes a discussion about the opening of an essay on the question "What matters?". It covers the original argument, the objections raised against it, suggested revisions, a map of metaethical positions, a proposed classification of those positions, and the relationship between value, minds, and consciousness.

---

### 1. The original opening: main claims

1. **Centrality.** The importance of any question rests on its relation to the fundamental question "What matters?", which asks whether there are objective value-facts and, if so, what they are: what is good or bad, what they are about, and how they compare.
2. **Stakes and neglect.** Mistaken views about what matters risk misapplied effort and harm. Yet there is little awareness of the problem and little effort to solve it.
3. **Unreliable belief formation.** Human beliefs about fundamental value are shaped mostly by upbringing, culture, and psychology, and are often inconsistent and uninformed by science and philosophy.
4. **Weak evidence.** Observational evidence has done little to narrow the answer; the question remains open in philosophy.
5. **The wager.** Even high certainty that there are no value-facts would not change how we should act. For any non-zero credence in value-facts, the best choice is the same.
6. **Is-ought.** "Should" is given no special metaphysical meaning: "an agent should make decision X" means "X would result in the best outcome."

---

### 2. Objections and problems identified

#### 2.1 The wager rests on a false dichotomy

The argument contrasts "objective value-facts exist" with "nothing matters." But many metaethical positions (subjectivism, constructivism, relativism, expressivism) deny objective value-facts without saying nothing matters. Unlike strict nihilism, some of these produce their own rankings of options, which can conflict with realist recommendations. So **credence in realism does affect what one should do**, unless these views are addressed separately.

#### 2.2 The wager has unstated premises

Even against strict nihilism, the argument assumes:

- ==**Expected-value maximization across metaethical hypotheses.** This is itself a normative principle, and it is hardest to justify at exactly this level.== _Response: Can this be clarified? Is it not something more general than expected-value maximization? Can we just enumerate the cases and see which choice is preferred in all of them without aggregation?_
- **Nihilism assigns equal value to all options.** ==It may instead leave value comparisons _undefined_, which does not drop out of an expected-value sum the way zero does.== _I don't understand this. Can "undefined" be a metaphisical/axiological thing, or can it just be epistemic?_
- **Tiny probabilities can dominate.** If credence in realism is very small, the argument enters "Pascal's mugging" territory, where many think decision rules need further defense. _I understand this is not a problem unlike in Pascal's mugging because nihilism is indifferent._

==The wager is defensible== (Jacob Ross argues for a version of it), but it is stronger if these premises are stated.
_Please state this version._

#### 2.3 Intertheoretic comparison

Expressivism does not claim there are value-facts of a different kind; it claims value statements are not fact-stating. It is unclear how to assign probability to such a hypothesis and compute expected value across it alongside realist hypotheses, since they share no common currency. This is part of the **problem of intertheoretic value comparison** in the moral uncertainty literature.

#### 2.4 The definition of "should" builds in consequentialism

Defining "should" as "results in the best outcome" (close to G. E. Moore's early view) assumes value is outcome-based, agent-neutral, and maximizing. Deontologists, virtue ethicists, and anyone accepting agent-relative reasons would reject the definition itself. A more neutral alternative: **"X is the option most favored by the true value-facts."**

#### 2.5 The is-ought gap is relocated, not removed

Knowing which decision yields the best outcome requires both descriptive facts and evaluative facts. Positing evaluative facts as a separate category of objective fact (non-naturalist or robust realism) is legitimate, but it makes the **epistemic question** central: if observation cannot narrow down value-facts, what can? Candidates include intuition, reflective equilibrium, and convergence among idealized reasoners.

#### 2.6 The debunking point cuts both ways

The observation that value beliefs are shaped by forces unrelated to truth can be turned against realism itself. Sharon Street's **Darwinian dilemma**: if evolution shaped our evaluative faculties without regard to objective value, either our value beliefs are unreliable (and value-facts unknowable), or value is closely tied to our attitudes (pushing toward anti-realism). A realist project needs a reply.

#### 2.7 The neglect claim is overstated

Metaethics, axiology, population ethics, and moral uncertainty are active research fields. "Little attention relative to the stakes" is more defensible than "not much trying."

#### 2.8 Self-reference

"Importance," "harm," and "misapplied effort" are evaluative notions that presuppose some answer to the question being raised. Not fatal, since any inquiry into value must start somewhere, but worth acknowledging.

### 3. Suggested revisions to the opening

| Paragraph | Main issues | Direction of revision |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Centrality and definition | Evaluative claim before value is introduced; equates "What matters?" with "Are there objective value-facts?"; "what they are about" is vague | Split the question into a **metaethical layer** (does anything matter objectively, and if not, in what sense?) and a **first-order layer** (what is good or bad, what makes it so, how goods compare) |
| 2. Stakes and inquiry | "Harm" presupposes value; neglect overstated; "inconsistent and uninformed" may read as dismissive; "observational evidence" implies an unnamed contrast | Frame neglect relative to stakes; name the kind of evidence that _could_ bear on value-facts |
| 3. Wager | False dichotomy; unstated premises; "act differently" from what?; "non-zero certainty" mixes terms | Define nihilism explicitly; state the expected-value premise; use "credence"; restrict the argument to nihilism and flag that stance-dependent views are handled separately |
| 4. Is-ought | Consequentialism built into the definition; gap relocated rather than bridged | Use "most favored by the true value-facts," or keep the original definition but flag it as an assumption; state that the problem becomes epistemic |

**General points:** use one spelling ("value-facts" or "value facts"); define "objective" early; define "nihilism" explicitly, since it is used both narrowly (error theory) and loosely (any denial of objective value); end with a roadmap showing where the essay goes next.

### 4. The metaethical landscape

#### 4.1 Realism as a bundle of commitments

Robust realism holds that value claims:

1. are truth-apt (can be true or false),
2. are sometimes true, and
3. are made true by facts independent of anyone's attitudes, beliefs, or choices.

**Anti-realism is the denial of realism**, and each anti-realist view rejects a different part of the bundle. Nihilism is one anti-realist view, not the whole category.

#### 4.2 The main positions

- **Error theory** (J. L. Mackie). Value claims aim at objective truth but are all false. Closest to "nothing matters."
- **Revisionism / fictionalism** (Mackie; Richard Joyce). Accept error theory, then keep using value terms in a revised sense or as a useful fiction.
- **Expressivism.** Value claims are not descriptions; they express attitudes. "Cruelty is wrong" is more like a stance than a report. Simon Blackburn's **quasi-realism** argues that expressivists can earn talk of moral truth and moral mistakes in a deflationary sense, and denies that ordinary moral talk contains any error.
- **Simple subjectivism.** "X is good" means "I approve of X": a true or false description of the speaker's psychology.
- **Idealized-desire subjectivism.** What is good for you depends on what you would want under full information and reflection. Unlike simple subjectivism, you can be mistaken about it. (Peter Railton and Michael Smith develop views in this family, though both describe their positions as forms of realism, which illustrates how blurry the terminology is.)
- **Relativism.** Value claims are true relative to a culture's or group's standards.
- **Constructivism** (Christine Korsgaard and others). Value-facts are real and binding but constituted by a procedure, such as what rational agents would agree to, or what follows from the standpoint of valuing anything at all. Kantian versions claim conclusions binding on every rational agent and sometimes call themselves minimally realist.

#### 4.3 Three regions rather than two

- **Robust realism:** things matter objectively.
- **Error theory:** nothing matters.
- **A large middle territory:** things matter, but in a way that depends on attitudes, perspectives, or procedures.

### 5. Classifying the views

#### 5.1 The proposed classification

1. Nihilism / error theory with value terms repurposed for descriptive meaning: expressivism, subjectivism(?)
2. Some things matter, but the metaphysics treats agents or other high-level concepts as fundamental: subjectivism(?), relativism, constructivism

#### 5.2 Refinements

- **The underlying instinct is sound.** It separates views that, from the realist's side, concede nothing really matters and keep the vocabulary, from views claiming things genuinely matter in a mind-dependent way.
- **Expressivism is not descriptive repurposing.** Its central claim is that value terms are _non_-descriptive. "Repurposing for descriptive meaning" fits **simple subjectivism**. Expressivism can still go in category 1, but because it denies realist value-facts, not because it redescribes them. Expressivists would object to the grouping with error theory.
- **"Fundamental" is the wrong word for category 2.** These views say value is _grounded in_ or _constituted by_ attitudes or procedures, not that agents are metaphysically basic. The standard term is **stance-dependent** or **mind-dependent**.
- **The sorting criterion is normative authority.** Ask: does the view say there are genuine reasons, facts about what one should do that can override what one currently wants?
  - No → category 1. Simple subjectivism lands here.
  - Yes → category 2. Idealized-desire subjectivism lands here.
  - So "subjectivism" names two different views, which is why it appeared in both categories.

#### 5.3 Revised classification

| Category | Views | What it says about reasons |
|---|---|---|
| **No genuine reasons** | Error theory, revisionism, fictionalism, simple subjectivism, (more contestably) expressivism | Value terms fail to refer, describe attitudes, or express them; nothing has normative authority in the essay's sense of "should" |
| **Stance-dependent reasons** | Idealized-desire subjectivism, relativism, constructivism | Genuine facts about what one should do exist, but are grounded in attitudes, cultures, or rational procedures |
| **Stance-independent reasons** | Robust realism | Facts about what one should do hold independently of any attitudes |

The boundary between rows 2 and 3 is also contested, especially for Kantian constructivism.

#### 5.4 Payoff for the wager

- **Category 1 behaves like nihilism** in the argument: under those hypotheses nothing matters in the essay's sense of "should," so they contribute equally to all options and drop out.
- **Category 2 does not drop out**, because it generates genuine and possibly conflicting rankings.
- The wager therefore becomes: set aside category 1; the real comparison is between **robust realism and stance-dependent views**. This is more precise than the original framing.
- **Caveat:** expressivists would say their view preserves everything that matters in practice, so the essay needs to defend why their notion of mattering does not count for its purposes.

### 6. Minds, fundamentality, and why minds would matter

#### 6.1 Most stance-dependent views are naturalist

Mainstream subjectivists and relativists accept that minds are physical systems and attitudes reduce, at least in principle, to lower-level processes. They do not posit souls or irreducible selves.

#### 6.2 The challenge: why should value track this high-level pattern?

If minds reduce to lower-level phenomena, why do they ground value and not some other pattern? Two standard replies:

- **Reduction is not elimination.** A hurricane is "nothing but" molecules, but "hurricane" picks out a real, causally important pattern. Valuing can be a real functional kind (Daniel Dennett's **"real patterns"**), and that kind, not its particular realization, grounds value.
- **"Mattering" is conceptually relational.** To matter is to matter _to_ something. If this analysis is right, value must attach to whatever cares, as a matter of what the concept means. Low-level physics cares about nothing, so it cannot be where value lives: not because it is less real, but because it lacks the relation.

#### 6.3 Costs of these replies

- **Vague boundaries of valuers.** Thermostats, bacteria, corporations, and ecosystems all have goal-directed states to some degree. Stance-dependent views need a non-arbitrary criterion for what counts as a valuer. Appealing to consciousness or sophisticated agency then requires explaining why those features matter **without smuggling in a stance-independent claim**.
- **Personal identity.** Idealized-desire views rely on what _you_ would want, assuming a persisting self whose idealized attitudes are authoritative. Derek Parfit (_Reasons and Persons_) argued that identity reduces to psychological continuity and connectedness, which come in degrees, and so loses much of its normative weight. This undercuts views grounding value in a particular agent's stance and pushes toward a more impersonal view.

#### 6.4 Views that do treat minds as special

- **Kantian constructivism.** Rational agency is privileged _from the practical point of view_, not in the inventory of what exists: in deliberating, one is already committed to treating something as a reason, and this standpoint cannot be escaped.
- **Phenomenal (hedonistic) realism** (Sharon Hewitt Rawlette, _The Feeling of Value_). The badness of pain is an intrinsic feature of the experience, not a matter of anyone's attitude toward it. Combined with the view that consciousness is irreducible (David Chalmers), this gives a principled reason why minds matter more than low-level phenomena: experience is where intrinsic, non-structural properties appear, and value may be one of them.

#### 6.5 Key distinction: mind-involving vs. stance-dependent

- **Mind-involving:** value concerns conscious states.
- **Stance-dependent:** value depends on attitudes.
- These are often conflated, but **phenomenal realism is mind-involving and stance-independent** at once. This gives a concrete answer to the essay's question of what value-facts are "about."

#### 6.6 Solipsism and agent-relativity

- No stance-dependent view is solipsist in the metaphysical sense; none denies other minds, and a subjectivist can care deeply about others.
- But **individual subjectivism leans toward agent-relativity**: each agent's reasons derive from their own attitudes, so there may be no fact about which outcome is better _simpliciter_, only better-for-A and better-for-B, with nothing settling conflicts. The nearest analogue to solipsism is "only perspectives exist, and none is privileged."
- Intersubjective views (constructivism, some relativism) are partly attempts to escape this.
- **For the essay:** agent-relativity is probably the strongest argument against stance-dependent views, at least for readers who think some outcomes are simply better than others, and the natural place to argue that the realist branch should dominate the wager.

### 7. Stances in consciousness vs. stances as components of consciousness

#### 7.1 The observation

Phenomenal consciousness seems metaphysically special: not describable without reference to experience (Chalmers). But many stances seem to be things that are (sometimes) **represented in** consciousness rather than **components of** consciousness. If so, the hard problem supports the specialness of experience, but not obviously of stances.

#### 7.2 In philosophy of mind

- **Phenomenal character vs. intentional content.** Desires and beliefs are standardly intentional states, and many are **dispositional**: you desire a friend's wellbeing while asleep, with nothing in consciousness. So most stances are at most occurrently represented in consciousness, not components of it.
- **Cognitive phenomenology** (Tim Bayne and Michelle Montague, eds., 2011). Do thinking and wanting have their own phenomenal character, or do only sensory and emotional states?
- **Phenomenal intentionality theory** (Terence Horgan and John Tienson; Uriah Kriegel; Angela Mendelovici). Genuine intentionality derives from phenomenal consciousness. If true, conscious stances are partly constituted by experience, and the distinction becomes one between **occurrent, felt attitudes** and **merely dispositional ones**.

#### 7.3 In value theory: the pleasure and pain debate

- **Felt-quality theories** (Rawlette; Roger Crisp). Pleasure and pain have a distinctive phenomenal tone, and their goodness or badness is intrinsic to it.
- **Attitudinal theories** (Fred Feldman; Chris Heathwood). An experience is pleasant because the subject likes or wants it.
- **The heterogeneity problem** motivates attitudinal theories: a hot bath, solving a proof, and hearing good news seem to share no common felt quality, so what unites them must be an attitude.
- **Empirical evidence: pain asymbolia.** Patients feel pain, describe its location and intensity, but do not mind it and show no aversion. Similar reports come from some patients on morphine or after certain lobotomies. Sensory and affective components of pain can come apart, raising the question of which one carries the badness. See Guy Kahane, "Pain, Dislike and Experience" (2009), and David Bain's work on evaluativist accounts of pain.

#### 7.4 A spectrum of where value might reside

| Location of value | Relation to consciousness | Support from irreducibility of consciousness |
|---|---|---|
| **Phenomenal valence** (felt badness as a feature of the experience) | Component of consciousness | Strongest; mind-involving but stance-independent |
| **Felt attitudes toward experience** (occurrent, conscious aversion) | Component of consciousness, attitudinal in form | Inherits whatever specialness experience has |
| **Represented or dispositional attitudes about the world** (e.g., desire-satisfaction theories; a desire about one's reputation frustrated after death) | At most represented in consciousness | None |

#### 7.5 Implications

- The line between stance-dependent views and phenomenal realism is blurrier than the classification in section 5 suggests. A view where value lies in felt aversion is attitudinal in form but may be **stance-independent in substance**, since the badness does not depend on any further attitude toward the aversion.
- A cleaner division for the essay may be: **value grounded in phenomenal states vs. value grounded in intentional states that need not be experienced.** Only the first is supported by the hard problem, matching the asymmetry noticed in 7.1.

---

### 8. Open questions for the essay

1. What kind of evidence, if not observation, could bear on value-facts?
2. How should the wager handle stance-dependent views, which do not drop out?
3. Can expected-value reasoning across metaethical hypotheses be justified, and how should undefined comparisons and tiny credences be treated?
4. How can intertheoretic comparison work when one hypothesis (expressivism) denies that value claims state facts?
5. Should "should" be defined neutrally, or should the essay commit to and defend consequentialism?
6. What is the reply to Street's Darwinian dilemma?
7. Is value grounded in phenomenal states, in intentional states, or both, and does the irreducibility of consciousness favor the first?
8. Does agent-relativity give a decisive reason to favor the realist branch?
